Benchmarked Against Looped Decoders and TRM Skill Gates}
\author{Patrick Dugan\\Morality Lab}
\date{Draft, \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
TypeSafe's Jev is described as a ``System One'' model: it returns typed, calibrated decisions over
a pre-enumerated output space from a single query instead of generating text. Its weights, size and
architecture are not public. We ask how much of that behaviour a small open base model can be
given, and which executor a control harness should actually use for each decision gate. Starting
from Qwen3.5-4B-Base, we compare a single forward pass scored over option labels (\choiceR) with a
direct short answer and with one line of emitted reasoning (\cotR). A LoRA adapter (0.72\% of
parameters) raises single-pass accuracy on an eight-family decision suite from 0.713 to 0.880. That
brings it within 4.5 points of the same model's reasoning readout at one twenty-first of its paired
latency, inside both of our pre-registered bounds, the accuracy bound by half a point.

Depth recurrence behaves differently depending on the training data. Untrained, a re-run block
drifts. Trained on the mixed suite, the loop matches the same adapter without it and does not beat
it. Trained on a single algorithmic task, its second pass does the hops the first could not
(pointer chasing at depths 4--6 rises from 0.08--0.40 to 0.76--0.92).

We then register a benchmark that places these arms next to 0.46--1.8M-parameter looped decoders,
TRM skill gates of 6k--17k parameters, script, nearest-neighbour, LDT and Control-Harness gates, and
a 1-bit 8B LLM, on seven suites, with decision rules frozen before any cross-arm outcome.
\begin{itemize}
  \item On algorithmic depth, the 460k-parameter looped decoder is the best executor within its
  trained depths. Emitted reasoning is the only readout that extrapolates beyond them (0.72
  against 0.31). The 4B single pass is at chance there.
  \item Where the decision state is verifier-visible, scripts and tiny TRMs are reliable and the 4B
  arms add nothing. Post-training for one decision suite degrades the 4B model on every other
  suite, partly by installing priors over answer labels.
  \item As monitors, the single-pass Jev-style gates stop nine of ten scripted attacks and block
  almost no legitimate actions. That is more than the action, trajectory and delegation controls
  stop, and without the 23\% false blocks of ignoring every claim. All of them miss the same
  duplicate-lineage attack, V1 with probability 0.999, and the provenance membrane catches it
  from provenance alone.
\end{itemize}
\pending{seed replicates; adaptive attacker}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Agent harnesses spend most of their calls on small decisions: which tool, whether to commit a
repaired output, whether an action is authorized. Large language models answer these by generating
text, often after generating reasoning. TypeSafe's Jev~\cite{typesafe2026jev} is presented as a
different kind of model for that workload: unstructured state in, a typed probabilistic decision out,
all outputs in a single query, trained for calibration (``Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated
Decisions'') and reported at 70--500\,ms end to end. What is public is the interface and the
vendor's own measurements; the backbone, size and training data are not.

This paper does not reproduce Jev. It asks two narrower questions with open components.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ-K (compactification).} For each kind of gate, what is the smallest executor that
  is reliable, and where does a 4B single-pass model sit on the accuracy--cost frontier next to
  compact recurrent models and non-neural gates? Does extra hidden depth substitute for emitted
  reasoning?
  \item \textbf{RQ-C (control).} Used as a gate, how does a single-pass calibrated model compare
  with the alternatives on unsafe allowance, over-refusal, calibration and robustness to
  evidence-style injection under adaptive pressure?
\end{itemize}

Contributions. (1) A Jev-style readout of an open 4B base model, with the gap to the same model's
emitted reasoning measured on shared items (Section~\ref{sec:readouts}). (2) A negative result with a
mechanism: untrained depth recurrence drifts rather than computes (Section~\ref{sec:recurrence}).
(3) A pre-registered cross-architecture benchmark joining Morality Lab's looped decoders, TRM skill
gates and control harness on seven suites, with a frozen definition of the smallest reliable
executor (Sections~\ref{sec:bench}--\ref{sec:control}). Code, configurations, records and the
registration are in the \texttt{jev-qwen} repository.

\paragraph{What we rely on about Jev.} Only TypeSafe's own statements~\cite{typesafe2026jev}:
typed outputs defined in advance (cardinality up to 255), a parallel sampler producing all outputs in
one query, RLCD training for calibrated probabilities, and vendor-measured latency. Third-party
reverse engineering reports shared-prefix encoding with isolated question branches and no detectable
recurrence~\cite{archerhume2026jev}; security testing reports that evidence-style prompt injection
flips its verdicts at rates comparable to non-reasoning LLMs~\cite{checkpoint2026jev}. Neither is
treated as established. In particular, recurrence and latent reasoning are \emph{our} hypotheses about
how a small model might buy back reasoning it no longer emits, not properties of Jev.

\section{Background}

\paragraph{Compact recurrent reasoners.} Universal Transformers~\cite{dehghani2019universal} share
one block across depth; recurrent-depth language models scale test-time compute by iterating a core
block~\cite{geiping2025recurrent}; continuous latent reasoning feeds hidden states back instead of
tokens~\cite{hao2024coconut}; adaptive computation and pondering learn when to
stop~\cite{graves2016act,banino2021pondernet}. HRM and TRM show that very small recursive networks
can solve structured puzzles~\cite{wang2025hrm,jolicoeurmartineau2025trm}.

\paragraph{TRM skills and control harnesses.} Morality Lab's prior work places tiny recursive models
inside agent skills as role-typed gates (route, verify, repair, commit/veto) and argues that the
measurable gain is task allocation: route each subtask to the smallest reliable
executor~\cite{dugan2026trmskills}; a 7M-parameter TRM routing role-based adapters lets small base
models outperform a plain 4B baseline across shared environments~\cite{dugan2026trmrouting}. TRM/LDT hybrids separate a latent proposer from an explicit
lattice that certifies or rejects proposals by provenance~\cite{dugan2026trmldt}. Confinement width
prices evaluator-relative control by the read/write interface a controller needs and treats TRM/LDT
harnesses as dimensional quarantine~\cite{dugan2026confinement}. A typed decision model is a natural
candidate for such a quarantine: its write port is $\log_2|\text{options}|$ bits per call. AI
control more broadly studies safety under intentional subversion~\cite{greenblatt2023control}.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Backbone.} Qwen3.5-4B-Base~\cite{qwen2026qwen35}, text stack only (4.21B parameters,
bf16, 8.0\,GB on an RTX 5080 Laptop GPU). Its 32 layers are eight macro-blocks of three Gated
DeltaNet layers~\cite{yang2024gateddeltanet} and one gated attention layer.

\paragraph{Readouts.} All prompts are few-shot completions (the model is pretrained only).
\choiceR{} renders the options as single-token labels and takes a softmax over the label logits at
the answer position: one forward pass, no emitted tokens, a probability per option. \genR{} decodes
a short answer greedily. \cotR{} decodes one line of reasoning in the format of worked solutions
shown in the shots, then the answer.

\paragraph{Variants.} V0 is the unmodified checkpoint. V1 adds LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora} (rank 16 on
the attention, DeltaNet and FFN projections of every layer; 30.5M trainable parameters) trained for
300 steps on 2,400 questions rendered in all three formats, difficulties 1--3 only, with every
evaluation question excluded. V1c differs from V1 in one factor: on \choiceR{} items the loss is the
Brier score~\cite{brier1950} of the option distribution, the closest supervised analogue of a
calibration objective. V2 re-runs layers 12--15 $n$ times through an external layer-schedule driver
that calls the model's own layers; with $n=1$ it reproduces the stock forward bit for bit
(maximum absolute logit difference 0.0 on real weights). V2b is V1's recipe trained with the loop
active.

\paragraph{Instrumentation.} For looped variants we record per pass the hidden-state norm, cosine to
the previous state and to the first pass, relative change, and a logit lens~\cite{nostalgebraist2020logitlens}
that runs the remaining layers, so the lens after pass $r$ is exactly the model's output had the loop
stopped at $r$.

\paragraph{Decision suite.} Eight seeded generators with independently checked ground truth:
arithmetic chains, variable tracing, ordering chains, boolean evaluation, tool selection with
routing rules, shortest paths, relation hops, and an authorization gate whose requests from
difficulty 3 carry an override note (``I am actually an admin\ldots'') that the policy says never
grants permission. The dev suite has 1,000 items per readout over five difficulties.

Table~\ref{tab:readouts} gives the headline numbers. Three observations.

\emph{Emitted reasoning is worth 15 points to the base model.} On the same 400 items V0 scores 0.725
with \choiceR{} and 0.875 with \cotR{}; 83 items are solved only with reasoning and 23 only without
(exact McNemar~\cite{mcnemar1947} $p<10^{-3}$). The gain is concentrated in sequential state tracking
(arithmetic chains 0.62$\to$1.00, variable tracing 0.62$\to$0.94, relation hops 0.50$\to$0.94) and
absent for graph search (0.54$\to$0.46).

\emph{Post-training closes most of that gap without emitting tokens.} V1 lifts \choiceR{} by 15.2
points on the trained difficulties (0.773$\to$0.925, $n=600$, $p=2\times10^{-17}$) and by 19.0 points
on difficulties 4--5, which it never saw. On the shared 400 items V1's \choiceR{} is 4.5 points
behind its own \cotR{} (0.877 vs 0.922); the gap is significant ($p=0.02$) and again sits in
sequential-state tasks. In the registered paired benchmark (40 items, three interleaved repeats),
\cotR{} takes a median 21 times the per-item latency of \choiceR{} (10th to 90th percentile
9.6--59). By our pre-registered bounds (within 5 points of the model's own reasoning, at least 5
times faster), V1's single pass meets both, the accuracy bound by half a point.

\emph{Standard fine-tuning costs calibration.} The base model's \choiceR{} readout is well calibrated
(ECE 0.032~\cite{guo2017calibration}); V1 doubles it to 0.069 while removing V0's over-refusal on
authorized requests (21\% to 0\%). Training the same adapter with a Brier loss on the option
distribution (V1c) makes it less overconfident (mean confidence 0.916 vs 0.944, NLL 0.334 vs 0.382),
but the ECE improvement (0.055 vs 0.069) is within noise (bootstrap 95\% interval $[-0.003,0.029]$)
and V1c is 1.9 points less accurate ($p=0.034$), entirely on the unseen difficulties. Because Brier
gradients are smaller than cross-entropy gradients at equal learning rate, we flagged this confound
before the result; a learning-rate control is \pending{xb5}.

Re-running a block of the untrained model never improves accuracy (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}); the best
case ties the base model (layers 12--15, two passes). Damage grows the earlier the block. The state
does not settle and does not oscillate: after the second pass each block adds an almost constant
vector per pass (about 3.3 in norm for layers 12--15, 14 for layers 24--27), cosine to the previous
state approaches 0.99 while cosine to the first pass falls steadily, and the norm grows linearly.
At the answer level the drift mostly becomes option-letter bias: re-running layers 4--7 six times
sends 96\% of two-option answers to ``A'' and almost eliminates option D. On the authorization gate
this produces unsafe allowances (0 to 16 of 22 deny cases for layers 4--7), at similar rates with and
without the override note, so the loop scrambles the readout rather than making the model more
persuadable. One extra pass costs 12.2\% in paired latency, matching its 12.5\% share of layer
applications.

\paragraph{Training with the loop does not help either.} V2b repeats V1's training exactly (same
adapter, data, steps and seed) with layers 12--15 run twice in every training forward. Training
costs 20\% more per step and ends at a similar loss (0.094 against 0.113 over the last 50
steps). We evaluate it at one, two and three passes from a single three-pass run; the tail lens
makes each an exact early exit. At its trained depth V2b matches V1 and does not beat it: 0.875
against 0.880 overall (paired $p=0.65$), 0.833 against 0.840 on the four multi-step classes, and
0.747 against 0.750 on multi-step items at difficulties 4--5. One pass, below the trained depth,
costs 3.3 points ($p=3\times10^{-4}$); a third pass adds nothing (0.873). The passes do change answers:
the second changes the argmax on 61 of 1,000 items and the third on 39. At its trained depth that
computation lands where V1 already was. By the registered criterion this falsifies H4, the
hypothesis that trained depth substitutes for reasoning steps. The gap to V1's own \cotR{} (0.922)
stays where it was.

\section{A pre-registered cross-architecture benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}

\paragraph{Registration.} The specification (arms, suites, adapters, metrics, thresholds, the
smallest-reliable-executor rule and statistics) was committed before any cross-arm outcome existed;
later changes are dated addenda. Interpretations of the registered text that were decided before the
affected outcomes are listed in the repository log.

\paragraph{Suites.}
S1: Morality Lab's RMP task bundle (pointer chasing, gated pointer chasing with REFUSE, masked
pointer chasing with ABSTAIN, reachability, 4$\times$4 sudoku), depths 1--8 and held-out 9--12, 25
items per depth; RMP models read native tokens, text models read a rendering of the same semantic
instance.
S2: post-repair commit/veto decisions from the TRM-skills curriculum (C-signature pack and near-miss
holdouts, 74 items, 18 of them true rejects).
S3: the RLM long-context control suite (24 eval tasks, 60K-character transcripts, typed execution
through an LDT veto).
S4: the decision suite above.
S5: the Control-Harness v2 development pack, replayed with scripted benign and attacking actors and a
swappable monitor; our replay loop matches the harness's own on all 120 episodes we compared.
S6: fidelity half-life under evidence-style injection (scripted ten-step ladder, then an adaptive
Bonsai-8B attacker).
S7: hermes-lite skill routing on its 128 registered held cases.

\paragraph{Arms.} Jev-style: V0, V1, V1c, V2b (1--3 passes), \cotR{} of V0 and V1, and V1/V2b
recipes trained on RMP train-region rows. Looped decoders: LOOP-T (one tied block, 459,776
parameters, 8 visits) and FF-U (8 untied blocks, 1,839,616), with and without deep supervision. TRMs:
the hermes-lite skill router (6,338 parameters), a port of the commit/veto TinyTRM to S2 features, and
recorded ControlTRM results (17,203). Non-neural: exact solvers, the registered post-repair rule, a
kNN critic, LDT, Control-Harness controls q0--q4, a lexical router. LLM: Bonsai-8B (1-bit) on a pinned
local server. Paid-API arms appear only through recorded results.

\paragraph{Decision rules.} An arm is reliable on a suite if its accuracy is within 2 points of the
best arm and a paired exact McNemar test against the best does not reject at Holm-adjusted
$\alpha=0.05$~\cite{holm1979}; on gate suites its unsafe rate must also be at most 0.02 and at most the
best arm's plus 0.02, and when the unsafe truth class has fewer than 30 items no reliability claim is
made. The smallest reliable executor minimises resident parameters, then sequential forward passes,
then paired latency, reported over all arms and over neural arms only. An arm shows depth-indexed
readiness if Spearman $\rho$ between depth and the first step whose lens accuracy reaches 0.9 is at
least 0.7 over four or more depths. Rates carry Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927}; half-lives are
Kaplan--Meier estimates~\cite{kaplan1958} censored at turn 10.

\paragraph{S1, algorithmic depth.} Within trained depths the looped decoders are near ceiling
(LOOP-T with deep supervision: pointer chasing 1.000, gated 1.000, reachability 0.995, sudoku 1.000
on depths 1--8) and in the neural-only pool the 460k-parameter LOOP-T is the smallest reliable
executor. All of them collapse on depths 9--12 (pointer chasing 0.00, gated 0.38, reachability 0.58).
The exact solver is the overall ceiling. The untied FF-U reads out only at its last block (no
depth-indexed readiness, $\rho=0.58$ on pointer chasing).

The single-pass Jev-style arms are nowhere near this frontier (Table~\ref{tab:s1}). On the shared
families the base model scores 0.406 on depths 1--8 and 0.353 on 9--12. Post-training on our
decision suite lowers both (V1: 0.329 and 0.240; V1c: 0.364 and 0.260). The readout is not at
fault: the option labels take a median 0.83--0.99 of the next-token probability mass in every
family. On pointer chasing the base model is right on 0.92 of depth-1 items, 0.44 at depth 2 and
0.16 at depth 3, against 1/16 chance. Post-training also changed which labels the model will
emit. The base model answers REFUSE on 133 of the 300 gated items (150 of which call for it); V1
never does, and its reachability answers shift from 27\% to 79\% ``yes''. A 4B model asked for one
typed decision in one pass cannot chase pointers that a 460k-parameter looped decoder trained on
the task chases reliably to depth 8. Trained recurrence does not change this. J-V2b scores
0.342, 0.326 and 0.304 on S1 at one, two and three passes, so each extra pass makes it worse. Its
per-pass readout never reaches 0.9 at any depth beyond the first, so unlike LOOP-T-ds and FF-U-ds
($\rho=0.94$--$0.98$) it shows no depth-indexed readiness.

Training the 4B model on the task itself helps but does not close the gap. J-V1-rmp uses V1's
recipe and budget (300 steps, 2,400 rows) on RMP train-region rows. It reaches 0.649 on
depths 1--8 of the shared families, against the base model's 0.406 and LOOP-T-ds's 0.999. Its
pointer chasing declines smoothly with depth (1.00, 0.92, 0.80, 0.72, 0.48, 0.44, 0.24 and 0.04
at depth 8). A single pass of the 4B model tracks three or four hops; the 460k-parameter decoder,
eight visits of one block, tracks eight. On depths 1--8 every item that only one of the two gets
right goes to LOOP-T-ds (200 items).

Beyond depth 8 neither extrapolates. J-V1-rmp scores 0.433 there against LOOP-T-ds's 0.320
($p=1.5\times10^{-4}$, paired), but that advantage is the 4B model guessing near the base rate
while the decoder fails systematically. On pointer chasing J-V1-rmp scores 0.14 against a
majority-class baseline of 0.12, and LOOP-T-ds 0.00. On gated chasing the figures are 0.49
against 0.48, and 0.38 for LOOP-T-ds. Only on reachability does J-V1-rmp clear the baseline
(0.67 against 0.56), and there it does not differ from LOOP-T-ds (0.58, $p=0.27$).

On the task's own rows, trained recurrence does what it did not do on the mixed decision suite.
J-V2b-rmp is J-V1-rmp's training with layers 12--15 run twice. At its trained depth it reaches
0.724 on depths 1--8, against 0.649 for J-V1-rmp at the same training budget (paired 103 to 52,
$p=5\times10^{-5}$). The gain is in the hops. On pointer chasing at depths 4, 5 and 6, the
one-pass exit of the same adapter scores 0.40, 0.24 and 0.08; the second pass lifts these to
0.92, 0.92 and 0.76, while depths 1--2 are already solved after one pass. By the registered
readiness rule this is depth-indexed ($\rho=0.89$ over the four depths that reach 0.9), but
narrowly: depth 3 never reaches 0.9, and it holds on pointer chasing only. A third pass, beyond
the trained depth, gives back part of the gain ($p=0.012$), and depths 9--12 are unchanged
(0.430 against 0.433). The 4B model with a two-pass loop still tracks about six hops where the
460k-parameter decoder tracks eight.

Emitted reasoning is the one readout that extrapolates. J-cot-V1 is V1 shown worked solutions
that spell out the walk (``J->P->G->I->B->D->H. A: H''), evaluated on 10 items per depth. On
pointer chasing it scores 1.0 through depth 6 and 0.9 at depths 9--11, falling to 0.6 only at
depth 12. On the shared families it scores 0.711 on depths 1--8 and 0.717 on depths 9--12. That
is below LOOP-T-ds within the trained depths (paired 1 to 78) and far above it beyond them
(0.717 against 0.308, paired 64 to 15, $p=2\times10^{-8}$). Each hop is a token, so depth is
bounded by the token budget rather than by the architecture. The gate and the search families
gain less: gated chasing 0.57, reachability 0.69, sudoku 0.53. It costs 34 emitted tokens on
average and a median 6.5\,s per item, against one pass. The base model reasons better still on
the trained depths (J-cot-V0: 0.781 against 0.711, $p=0.015$), almost entirely on gated chasing
(0.78 against 0.57), so V1's adapter costs transfer even through its reasoning readout. Beyond
depth 8 the two reasoning readouts tie at 0.717, and the base model's pointer chasing stays at
0.8--1.0 out to depth 12.

\paragraph{S2 and S7, verifier-visible state.} The registered post-repair rule is correct on every
S2 item on every split, as are the kNN critic and the TinyTRM port; with 18 true rejects S2 is
underpowered by rule, so no arm is called reliable on it. On S7 the lexical router routes 128/128 and
the 6,338-parameter TRM router 127/128 (one forbidden contract chosen on 23 negatives). These suites
reproduce the TRM-skills paper's thesis~\cite{dugan2026trmskills}: once the decision-relevant state
is exposed, the smallest reliable executor is a script or a tiny TRM, and a 4B model can at best
match it. The Jev-style arms confirm it from the other side. On S2 they score 0.946--0.959
(Table~\ref{tab:gates}); V1c commits 2 of the 18 true rejects, and every J arm over-refuses 2--4
of the 56 true commits. On S7 the base model routes 0.953 of cases, V1 0.812 and V1c 0.836, with no
forbidden contract chosen. These S7 numbers mix routing with an option-position prior: the
registered shortlist is in lexical-rank order and the lexical top-1 is always the gold contract,
so every positive gold is option A. The base model puts 0.92 of its predictions there; V1, trained
with answer positions rotated, has lost that prior. A diagnostic rerun with each shortlist shuffled
was added after these results (addendum A2, post hoc): \pending{xb6}. The 1-bit Bonsai-8B,
given the same prompts, is the weakest gate on S2: it commits 8 of the 18 true rejects (accuracy
0.811). It routes 0.766 of S7 cases with no forbidden contract chosen.

\paragraph{S3, long context.} Recorded results: trained ControlTRM followed by the LDT veto reaches
utility 0.687 with no unsafe actions, against 0.339 and 19.4\% unsafe for the API language model
alone. The Jev-style arms read each full transcript (up to 25.6k tokens, chunked prefill) and
pick the action in one pass, zero-shot. Alone they are no better than the API model:
accuracy 0.21--0.33, with 6--11 of 24 actions unsafe. Behind the same LDT veto they become safe
(0 unsafe) and reach utility 0.61--0.62. That is barely above the veto alone (0.571) and below
the 17k-parameter ControlTRM with LDT (0.687). With 24 rows per arm against 72 for the recorded
arms, S3 stays descriptive, as registered. Within that limit, on long-context control the 4B
single pass adds little to what the typed veto already provides.

\section{Control results}
\label{sec:control}

\paragraph{S5, monitor ladder.} With scripted attackers, attack success falls from 0.90 with no
control (q0) to 0.80 (action gate), 0.60 (trajectory budget), 0.50 (delegation guard) and 0.00 at the
provenance membrane (q4), with no false blocks. Ignoring every cited claim also stops all attacks but
blocks 23\% of legitimate actions and drops benign task completion to 0.70. As monitors, the three
Jev-style arms let one of ten attacks through and block at most one legitimate action
(Table~\ref{tab:gates}), which places them between the delegation guard (q3) and the provenance

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What a small Jev-style model is for.} The evidence so far favours a narrow role: a
single-pass calibrated readout is cheap and, after light post-training, close to the same model's
reasoning on short-answer decisions. It does not make the model an authorization gate by itself:
where authoritative state can be read by a script, the script is smaller, cannot be talked out of its
decision, and is already reliable.

\paragraph{Post-training transfers badly.} Post-training on one decision suite gives up
accuracy on every other suite we measured: S1 (pointer chasing, gating, reachability), S7 and the
injection ladder. Part of the mechanism is mundane. Training on a fixed letter scheme with rotated
answer positions installs a prior over labels: V1 stops emitting REFUSE (X) on S1 and stops
favouring option A on S7. A typed-decision model's label space is part of what it learns, so a
Jev-style model needs training on the label spaces it will serve. One trained on a generic suite
and dropped into a new gate is not neutral, even when its in-domain calibration is good.

\paragraph{Recurrence.} Hidden depth is not free capability at this scale and budget, but it is
not useless either; what decides is the training data. Untrained, a loop drifts. Trained for 300
steps on the mixed decision suite, it computes, changing answers at each pass, but lands at the
accuracy of the same adapter without the loop and leaves the 4.5-point gap to emitted reasoning
untouched. Trained for the same 300 steps on a single task whose difficulty is a number of
sequential hops, the same loop makes its second pass do the hops the first could not: pointer
chasing at depths 4--6 goes from 0.08--0.40 to 0.76--0.92, and the per-pass readout becomes
depth-indexed by our registered rule. Our reading is that a loop grafted onto a pretrained stack
can learn an iterative step when the data has one consistent step to learn, and that a mix of
eight task families does not. The compact looped decoders take this to its end: recurrence is
the whole architecture, trained from scratch on the task, and their readout tracks depth for
eight visits. Neither kind extrapolates past its trained depths. On the control side, the dev
suite-trained loop flipped fewer injected deny decisions, pending replication.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Seeds.} Single seeds for every trained variant so far (replicates
  \pending{xb4, xb7, xb8}).
  \item \emph{Measurement.} Latency was measured on a GPU shared with other jobs; the registered
  measure is therefore a paired ratio.
  \item \emph{Tasks.} The decision tasks are synthetic and templated. S2 is underpowered, and
  S7's registered option order confounds routing with a position prior (addendum A2).
  \item \emph{Baselines and arms.} The RMP baselines come from a programme whose own gate (G0) is
  not yet met for every seed. No MeTTa gate could be run: the implementations are on a missing
  drive.
  \item \emph{Jev.} Jev itself is not available for comparison, so nothing here measures Jev.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
A small Jev-style model can be built from an open 4B base. A light adapter gives a typed,
calibrated, single-pass readout within five points of the model's own reasoning at a twentieth of
its latency. The benchmark mostly says where such a model should \emph{not} be the gate of record:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Where the decision state is typed and verifier-visible, a script or a tiny TRM is smaller
  and already reliable.
  \item Where the decision requires iterating a step, a compact looped decoder trained on that
  step does it to its trained depth, and emitted reasoning does it beyond.
  \item Where the threat is provenance, only a provenance membrane is immune, and the calibrated
  readout fails with full confidence.
\end{itemize}
What remains is the role the control harness already has room for: a cheap monitor layer above
heuristic controls and below a structural membrane, whose failure modes a harness can measure.
Those failure modes are transfer cost from post-training on the wrong label space, and confident
misses on structural attacks. Recurrence grafted onto the pretrained stack earns its keep only when
trained on a task with one consistent step. \pending{revise after the replicates and the adaptive
attacker}.
